# Chapter 4 — 2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz (and 6 GHz, briefly)

You've probably seen two network names at your Wi-Fi picker, or a setting in your router that asks which band to enable. Here's what's actually going on, in one sentence:

> **2.4 GHz goes farther but is slower and crowded; 5 GHz is faster but crowds out at distance and loses to walls.**

That's the trade. Everything else is detail.

## The two bands in a table

| | 2.4 GHz | 5 GHz |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Range | Farther | Shorter |
| Speed through walls | Survives a wall | Often doesn't |
| Top real-world speed | Low (rarely over ~100 Mbps) | High (150 Mbps to 1000+ Mbps) |
| How crowded | Very (microwaves, Bluetooth, neighbors) | Mostly empty |
| Interference from microwave | Yes, really | Minimal |
| Recommended for | IoT, remote rooms, older devices | Phones, laptops, TVs, streaming |

## The plain-English version

2.4 GHz is like FM radio: it travels well and battles through obstacles, but it's an older, slower technology, and in most homes it's being shared with a dozen other routers, a hundred Bluetooth devices, and a kitchen microwave.

5 GHz is like a nice short-throw speaker: faster, clearer, but it doesn't punch through the next room.

So:

- **Your phone and your laptop:** 5 GHz, whenever they support it.
- **Your TV or streaming box in the living room:** 5 GHz, if it's in a different room from the router, only if a cable test shows it holds up; ethernet cable for TV if you have one.
- **Your smart plugs, thermostat, color bulbs, doorbell:** 2.4 GHz. Many of these literally *cannot connect* to 5 GHz — they were built cheap on purpose.
- **A device that's really far away, on the other side of two walls:** 2.4 GHz. It'll be slow but way more reliable there.

## "But my Wi-Fi picker shows only one network"

Many modern routers merge both bands under one network name — you don't pick, the router decides per device. This is often called **band steering** or a **smart/merged network**.

- That's fine, and for most houses it's the correct setup. The router is usually good at sending the right device to the right band.
- But it has a limit: sometimes the steering is wrong. Your laptop gets pushed onto 2.4 GHz in the far bedroom and you grumble about slow Wi-Fi when it could have had 5 GHz.

### How to know if your phone is on 2.4 or 5

On your phone, open the Wi-Fi details for your connected network. You'll see something like:

- **"Frequency: 2.4 GHz"** or **"5 GHz"** (Android shows this clearly).
- **iPhone:** tap the (i) next to the network. The field is subtle; you may need a free app like Airport Utility to check the channel.

Alternatively: your router may expose two separate networks (like `Home-2G` and `Home-5G`). In that case, pick manually.

### How to force 5 GHz (if you care)

If you suspect your phone or laptop is on the wrong band:

1. Sign into your router admin page (see [chapter 9](09-router-admin-page.md)).
2. Look for "**Wireless**" or "**Wi-Fi**" settings.
3. Find the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz sections. See if your network name is **shared** or **split**.
4. If you want to experiment, you can give the 5 GHz band a slightly different name (e.g. `House_5G`). Then your good devices connect explicitly to that network. Your smart devices continue to see both, but they tend to pick 2.4 GHz, which is fine for them.
5. Devices that "prefer" 5 GHz will switch when you've explicitly joined the 5 GHz network.

Doing this loses some convenience (you now have two networks to manage), but it wins back control. Keep the names close to each other (like `Home_5` and `Home`) so guests don't get confused.

## 6 GHz — mentioned, then dismissed

Newer routers (mostly "Wi-Fi 6E" and "Wi-Fi 7" models) have a **6 GHz** band. It's even faster and less crowded than 5 GHz. If you have a 6 GHz-capable router and a recent phone, you may see it — and your devices will connect automatically. You don't need to do anything about it.

Don't feel like you're missing something by not buying a Wi-Fi 7 router. For most houses, most streaming, most everything you do, 5 GHz is still fully sufficient. Upgrade your *placement and channel* and you get more real-world improvement for $0.

## The one-sentence rule to remember

> If you're close to the router, you should be on 5 GHz. If you're far away or your device is a low-power gadget, it belongs on 2.4 GHz — and there's nothing wrong with that.
